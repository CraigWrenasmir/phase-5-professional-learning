### Slide 11

That brings us to what it means to be neurodiversity affirming. The starting point is that differences are not deficits. There are different ways of knowing, doing, being and belonging, and everyone has strengths and experiences challenges.

That means allowing for different ways of thinking, learning and taking part. When we look at belonging, for instance, we can stay open to the ways a student experiences connection, rather than expecting it to look the same for everyone.

In a classroom, it's easy for the things that need our immediate attention to fill the whole picture. A difficult transition. A task that hasn't started. A moment when joining the group has become too much. Those things matter. We need to understand them and provide support.

And we need to keep the rest of the person visible. What interests them? What brings them joy? How do they connect? What can they do that we might miss if we're only watching for difficulty?

### Slide 12

The social model helps us put that curiosity to work. On this slide, we're using an environment-first lens. We look for barriers in the place, the task, the routine and the expectations. Then we ask what can change around the student.

Here's an imaginary classroom example. A class is gathering on the floor for a story. The usual expectation is that everyone sits close together, in the same space, for the whole reading. One student finds that arrangement difficult.

Before deciding what the student needs to do differently, we can look at what this routine asks of them. How crowded is the space? What choices are there about where to sit? Is sitting in that particular place necessary to take part in the story?

We might explore another place to sit, or a different way of joining. Those are possibilities to investigate with the student. We haven't established what will help just by coming up with an option.

The next two steps on the slide matter: include the student's communication and experience, and review whether the change makes participation more workable. What can the student tell or show us about the change? Is it helping them take part?

Changing an environment won't make every difficulty disappear. It gives us somewhere useful to begin, with barriers and supports that we can notice, discuss and review.

### Slide 16

Autism can be understood as difference and as a dynamic disability. Holding both ideas matters. Recognising difference and strengths does not mean overlooking difficulty or the need for substantial support.

Access and support needs can change with the task, the environment, relationships and the capacity available at that time. Something a student can do in one setting, or on one day, may not be available in another. We need to look at what is happening around the student as well as what the task is asking of them.

That takes us away from a fixed judgement about what someone can always do. It invites a more useful question: what is making participation possible here, and what support is needed now?

Keep the individual in view. There isn't one pattern that will explain every Autistic student's experience. Strengths, difficulties and fluctuating capacity all belong in the picture we build with the student and the people who know them.

### Slide 4

### Slide 9

This slide introduces four sensory response patterns from occupational therapist Winnie Dunn's framework. You may encounter this language in an occupational therapy report or a Sensory Profile School Companion questionnaire. The patterns are useful lenses, rather than fixed types of person.

Registration describes a pattern of noticing less sensory information or missing cues that others notice. Seeking describes actively looking for more input, perhaps through movement, touch or sound.

Sensitivity describes noticing sensory input readily and being affected by it, without necessarily taking action to reduce it. Avoiding describes actively trying to reduce input, perhaps by moving away from a crowd or covering the ears.

The slide also uses under-responsive and over-responsive language, and distinguishes more passive responses from active ways of changing the input. Keep that distinction connected to the student's experience, rather than treating the labels as a judgement.

A student may show different patterns for different senses. Their responses may also change with the context, the load and the day. We are looking at nervous-system responses, not simply a decision to cooperate or avoid a task.

So use the framework to sharpen your questions. What input is present? What has the student communicated? What pattern might be worth exploring? An observation gives us a starting point, not certainty about what is happening inside.

### Slide 13

Think for a moment about your own attention. Does it move across many topics, or settle deeply into one? That question introduces monotropism: a framework for understanding attention focused deeply on fewer things at a time.

### Slide 4

There are two parts to keep distinct here: understanding and expressing. Understanding is information coming in and making sense. That might be a spoken instruction, a written sequence, a demonstration, or a gesture in context. Expressing is a message going out: an idea, a feeling, a question or a choice, through any communication method.
At school, a primary student may receive much of this information from one teacher, while a secondary student may receive it from several teachers across the day. The forms and expectations can change between lessons.
These don’t necessarily look the same for one person. Someone may speak at length and still need support to understand spoken information. Someone else may understand much more than they can readily express. Neither fluent speech nor limited speech gives us the whole picture. Communication can also change with the person, the place, the demands and how someone is feeling.
A A C means augmentative and alternative communication. It can include gesture, signs, a communication board or book, typing, or a communication device. Some people use it alongside speech and some instead of speech.
Repeated or scripted words can carry meaning too. Listen for the context and possible purpose before correcting the form. We don’t need to assign a language-processing label before respecting a student’s communication. If we need an individual assessment, we involve the relevant team. For our classroom practice, the question is: what does this environment help the student understand, and what ways does it give them to respond?

### Slide 8

### Slide 10

### Slide 15

Think of a student’s available energy as a battery. Each day starts with some charge, and it isn’t necessarily the same amount as yesterday. Activities and demands draw on that energy. As the battery runs lower, managing the next demand can become harder.
You may also hear people in the Autistic community talk about spoons. The idea is having a limited supply available: an action uses one spoon or several, and when spoons are low, there is less left for the next thing. These are ways of talking about capacity, rather than a measure we can calculate for someone else.
The other part of the picture is recharging. The right supports can reduce demand and help restore some capacity. A pause, useful information, a preferred way to communicate or a safe connection may make a difference, depending on the person.
So we’ll look for both sides in a school day: what draws on capacity, and what helps to restore it. What helps one student may be different for another.

### Slide 19

Support is a continuum, rather than one intervention that answers every need. Start with an inclusive environment: predictable routines, clear expectations, accessible information, and a classroom culture where different ways of participating are accepted and valued.
Your day-one setup will mainly work in this part of the continuum and the next: universal supports available to many students. Instructions can be offered in spoken, written and visual forms. ### Slide 2

Belonging, capacity and joy bring the threads of today together. Keep all three in the picture when you consider whether a change is helping a student flourish.
Belonging means being recognised, included and able to participate as oneself. Capacity asks us to consider what the day costs, what it protects and what helps restore energy. Joy includes interests, connection and authentic expression.
These ideas connect our neurodiversity-affirming lens, our classroom design and our partnerships. We begin with the person, learn about their experience, and bring student, family and school knowledge into the decisions.
Look again at the day-one setup you developed in Session Four. It may reduce a barrier to participation. Could it also support belonging or joy? How might it affect what the day asks of the student? Remember the questions from Session Three: what costs, and what lightens?
Academic progress can be part of the picture, but it won't tell us everything about a student's experience. Quiet compliance or doing something without support is not, by itself, evidence of flourishing. Keep returning to the student, and to belonging, capacity and joy, as you judge what your change is making possible.
