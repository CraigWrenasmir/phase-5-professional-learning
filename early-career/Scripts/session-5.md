### Slide 3

These six principles come from the Family-School Partnerships Framework. Let's connect each one to what we do.
First, parents and families are children's first and continuing educators. Their knowledge remains valuable when a child enters school. Second, learning is lifelong and happens across many settings. The classroom gives us an important view, but it is one view.
Third, partnerships are strengthened when the diversity and strengths of families are valued. We need to learn what those strengths are, rather than assuming every family can contribute in the same way. Fourth, community engagement can expand the people, knowledge and resources involved in learning.
Fifth, partnerships grow through mutual trust, respect and responsibility. We can show those qualities by listening carefully, explaining clearly and doing what we agree to do. Sixth, partnership needs committed, collaborative and creative leadership. Someone needs to make the time and processes possible.
In a meeting, these principles become visible through a shared purpose, student communication, accessible information, and clear next steps. Who will do what, and when will we check in again?
Family and school perspectives may differ. We can listen and show respect while we are still working through those differences. Bringing those perspectives together from the beginning gives us a better basis for deciding what to try.

### Slide 4

Let's bring those principles together. Shared goals help us understand what we're working towards, and shared values help us understand why it matters. Mutual trust and respect need to be present in the way we speak and listen.
Consulting on decisions means leaving room for what we hear to influence the decision. Seeing another perspective and inviting curiosity help us notice what our own position might hide. Centring student voice reminds us whose experience this work concerns.
Assuming good intent gives us a constructive starting point. We still need to attend to the effects of our actions, and to concerns someone raises. A useful question is, what can we learn from each other that will help this student? These principles apply whoever is at the table.

### Slide 5

Start with where the family is now. Some families are new to autism, new terms and new services. Others have years of experience and know a great deal about what supports their child. We need to find out rather than assume.
A first meeting with a new teacher can bring earlier school experiences back into the room. A new year, a move to a different school or the beginning of secondary school may involve uncertainty as well as possibility.
What might a family be carrying into that first meeting? Take a quiet moment to consider the question without attaching it to a particular family. There may be hopes, questions, practical demands or previous experiences you haven’t heard about yet.
A clear purpose, plain language, time for questions and a next step everyone understands can help at any stage. You don’t need every answer before the conversation. You can be prepared, be honest about what you are still finding out, and make a clear plan to follow up.
Keep that in mind when we practise the opening of a conversation shortly. The aim is to understand where this family or student is now, and what matters to them.

### Slide 6

The seesaw invites us to notice power between school, home and the student. Power can sit in income and resources, past experiences of education, culture and language, or knowledge of school systems and curriculum. It can also sit in what each person believes about school and professional authority.
We work within education every day. Its language and routines can feel ordinary to us, while being unfamiliar or intimidating to someone else. Think about an agenda full of acronyms, a meeting time that is hard to attend, or a decision presented as already settled.
Ask yourself: who sets the agenda? Whose knowledge is heard? Who can make decisions? Is the student included in a way that works for them? And what could the school change about the process?
These questions help us examine practice. They aren't an invitation to assign blame to an individual. Before describing a family as difficult or disengaged, we need to look carefully at the context, the relationship and the opportunities they have been offered to contribute. Our intention matters, and so does how the process is experienced.

### Slide 8

Students need a say in decisions about them, in ways that work with their communication. Even a young child can express a preference. We can offer visual choices, attend to a response, and honour what it tells us.
Questions such as, what helps, what's hard, and what would you like teachers to know, can open up that understanding. We may need to change the format, the setting or the time we allow for an answer.
Where a student uses words, record their words accurately. Where they communicate in other ways, make clear what they communicated and how. Keep adult interpretation visible as interpretation. Including their perspective in the record, and acting on it, helps build a foundation for self-advocacy.

Turn to The first two minutes on page twenty-four. Find a partner. One person will be the teacher, and the other will play a parent or a student. This is a low-stakes practice in asking and listening back before solving.

### Slide 11

Meeting families where they are begins with listening before solving. Ask about communication preferences and priorities. Make room for questions and uncertainty, including questions we haven't anticipated.
Explain roles, options and next steps in plain language. Check what is workable for the family now. A plan that looks simple within a school timetable may interact with demands we don't know about. This brings our environment-first thinking into the meeting itself. Where might the school's language, timing or process create a barrier? What could we change? A different format, questions in advance, a quieter place, or clearer follow-up may change the experience of consultation.
Keep asking what you need to understand. Listening and clarity give us a stronger basis for solving something together.

### Slide 12

On page twenty-five you'll find the Consultation checklist. You can use it before a meeting, and return to it afterwards.
Start with a clear, shared purpose. What are we trying to understand or decide together? Use open questions that allow an answer you haven't already chosen. Provide more than one way to contribute, and respect the knowledge the student and family bring.
Record what you hear accurately. It can help to check your understanding aloud: have I understood that correctly? Then agree an action, who is responsible and how you will follow up. If something remains undecided, make that clear too.
Afterwards, ask yourself whether the consultation changed or extended your understanding. Could everyone contribute in a workable way? Do people know what happens next?
Ask, listen and follow up. A checklist or planning tool can help us remember those steps, while the quality of the consultation still depends on our attention to the people involved.

### Slide 13

Let's look first at consulting students. Begin by presuming competence, and make communication accessible. That might involve augmentative and alternative communication, visuals, writing, drawing, video or symbols. Offer questions and meeting information beforehand, simple choices where useful, and enough processing time.
Notice the sensory environment. Build on the student's strengths and interests. Behaviour may communicate preferences or difficulty, so observe carefully and check your interpretation wherever possible. Trusted supporters can assist communication while keeping the student's own perspective central. What a student tells us needs to influence what we do.
With families, begin with the student's strengths, interests and what works. Listen to their knowledge without assuming you already know their experience. Use plain language, explain school terms and respect differences in culture and perspective. Some parents may be neurodivergent themselves, so check communication preferences rather than making assumptions.
Use real examples from school to discuss needs, goals, participation and possible adjustments. Check shared understanding, agree next steps and keep the conversation open as circumstances change.

### Slide 14

### Slide 15

Partnership becomes particularly important when a student's capacity runs out. The terms on this slide are meltdown, shutdown, and what the slide calls School Can't. That is, the student cannot attend school. We are considering these through capacity, accumulated demands and the parts of experience that may be difficult for others to see.
These experiences need a response without blame. Describing them as wilful misbehaviour, or as a failure of parenting, can close down the understanding we need. Home and school may see different parts of the student's day. A difference between those observations is a reason to listen and learn more.
Bring back the questions from Sessions Two and Three. What has the day been asking of this student? What may have accumulated? What information does the student have, and what might their family know about recovery?
Keep the focus on safety, what preceded the situation, what supports recovery, and what can change in the environment, task or response. Follow your school’s wellbeing, safety and support processes and involve the appropriate staff. Return to the contacts and pathway you identified in Session Three. A new teacher does not need to resolve this alone. The partnership task here is to share information respectfully, listen carefully and identify changes that may reduce demands and improve the student's experience.

### Slide 17

The NCCD Meetings Toolkit is another practical resource. NCCD stands for the Nationally Consistent Collection of Data on School Students with Disability. You can find the toolkit on the official NCCD website.
Its sections support requesting and managing meetings, being prepared, managing emotions, keeping records and considering whether a meeting was successful.
Use it to make the purpose of a meeting, the consultation process, possible adjustments, evidence and next steps clear. Explain responsibilities and follow-up in a way that the student and family can understand. People need to know why a meeting is happening, who is involved and how they can contribute.
You may contribute to these meetings without leading them. Find out your role, who makes the relevant decisions and who can help with your questions.
Follow the processes used in your school or system, and use current official NCCD information when you need specific guidance. The toolkit gives you a way to prepare and review the process while keeping the discussion connected to the student's experience.
