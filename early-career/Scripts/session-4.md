## Learning Design: Build it in from day one

### Slide 1

Welcome back to Learning Design. You’ll find the session summary on page seventeen. Have your school-day map and support map from Session Three nearby.
This session is about what you can build into your classroom from day one. Each of you will design your own setup, with a trio to help you sharpen and check it. You’ll decide what can be ready at the start, what will build across the term, and what you need to ask other people about.
Begin with the students you have in mind and the access questions on your day map. What does the environment, task or routine ask of them? What already helps? What might be clearer or more workable?
The setup will be something to try, review and refine with students, families and colleagues. Keep your questions open and your support map available. Knowing who to ask is part of the design.

Students come first. Before choosing a strategy, return to the real students and the evidence on your day map. What matters to them? What is the specific access question? What have you observed, and what are you still wondering?

### Slide 3

Early in your career, you’re choosing classroom habits that may stay with you. This is a useful time to build access into those habits. Some things can be prepared before a student has to ask for them.
Think about a schedule you refer to every day, a consistent cue for gaining attention, or a transition routine that is explained and taught. A calm space can be somewhere students choose to use, rather than a consequence or a place they are sent.
Predictability can help the teacher too, especially while you are still learning how a school works. A clear starting routine means you and the students have useful information about what will happen next.
Day one doesn’t have to mean the first day of a new school year. It can be the first day of next term, your next placement or your next relief booking. For a relief teacher, the setup might be the lesson plan written up at the start, an attention cue and a clear finishing routine.
You don’t need to set up everything at once. As you listen, hold one question: what could be in place in my classroom on day one that would make access easier?

### Slide 4

Some ideas about the perfect classroom arrive with the job. We’ve seen them on placement, in colleagues’ rooms or in resources. This slide invites us to look at those practices carefully.
There is plenty worth keeping: clear structure, warmth, explicitly taught routines and high expectations. Those can help students understand the environment and know they are welcome in it.
Then look again at practices such as sticker charts, public behaviour displays, very busy walls and routines that rely on everyone responding in the same way. Ask what the practice is for, who can succeed within it, and what it costs a student who cannot.
A very busy wall may add sensory demand throughout a day. A public display may make a student’s regulation visible to the whole class. Those are reasons to examine how the practice is experienced, including by the students for whom participation is hardest.
Keep this discussion respectful. Colleagues may use these systems thoughtfully and may know context you haven’t encountered yet. Look at the practice and ask useful questions. Separate what is required at your school from what is customary and what you can influence in your own room. Where a decision sits beyond your role, take the question to your mentor or leader.
Return to the day map: which habits support access, and which might add cost?

### Slide 10

Executive functioning supports can make the work of starting, remembering, sequencing, organising and shifting easier to manage. Start with the demand in your routine, then ask what would help with that demand.
If getting started is difficult, could the first step be visible and clear? If the task asks students to hold several instructions in mind, could the sequence stay available to return to? If organising time and materials takes a great deal of effort, could the task make those easier to locate and manage? If the challenge is moving between tasks, what information or preparation would make the shift clearer?
These are support examples to consider, not a package every student needs. Look back at your day map and brief. Which demand are you actually addressing? Would the support reduce unnecessary effort, or add another thing the student must manage?
Then consider how the student can show or tell you whether it helps. A resource can look organised to an adult and still be confusing or burdensome for its intended user. We’re looking for better access and participation, with the student’s experience helping us judge usefulness. More compliance pressure is not the aim.

### Slide 11

Predictability includes accessible information about both routine and change. We can’t promise every day will be the same. We can make information easier to find when a plan changes.
Think about how much classroom information is spoken once, implied, or kept in an adult’s head. If I couldn’t rely on remembering what someone told me, how would I know what was happening here?
Use the four questions on the slide. What is happening? What is changing? What comes next? What can I do if the plan changes? Give that information early enough to be useful.
In primary, perhaps assembly moves earlier. In secondary, a lesson might move to another room or be taken by a relief teacher. How will students find out? Is the change shown somewhere they can return to? Can they see what follows and ask a question or request support in a way that works for them?
A new teacher is already a change for students. Explaining how the class will run helps from the first lesson. Return to your day map and brief: where could you make information about routine and change more available?

### Slide 12

A schedule makes sequence, timing and change visible. It can cover a first-then sequence, a single lesson, part of the day or the whole day. Choose a format the intended students can understand and access.
There’s a difference between a schedule that is displayed and one that is used. Refer to it at the start, at changes of activity and when plans change. A primary classroom might have a whole-day schedule by the door. In secondary or relief teaching, you might put the lesson plan on the board and refer back to it as you move through the lesson.
Check whether the schedule shows students’ actual day, including changes and opportunities to pause. If it only lists the tasks adults want completed, it may leave important questions unanswered.
The example image on this slide is still a placeholder in the supplied deck. As you consider an example from your own setting, ask the question at the bottom: does the schedule still provide useful information when the day changes?
Its value comes from reducing uncertainty and supporting participation. Watch it in use, invite feedback, and adjust it as you learn what students need.

### Slide 13

With any visual support, start by asking what the student needs to find, understand, remember or communicate. Then choose a form that can make that information clear. It might be an object, photograph, symbol, written word, checklist, map or choice support.
Visual does not automatically mean accessible. The student needs to understand what it represents and be able to use it at the point of need. Keep unnecessary detail out, explain how it works, and check its meaning with the student.
Page nineteen gives four quick-review questions. Can the student understand it? Can they access it when they need it? Does it reflect what is actually happening, including change? Does it give useful information or a way to respond?
Keep understanding and expressing in view. A visual support may help someone understand, while A A C provides ways to communicate. Access to A A C doesn’t mean every spoken instruction or schedule is understood. Keep the student’s communication system available during routines, transitions and changes, and recognise responses beyond speech.

### Slide 19

Support is a continuum, rather than one intervention that answers every need. Start with an inclusive environment: predictable routines, clear expectations, accessible information, and a classroom culture where different ways of participating are accepted and valued.
Your day-one setup will mainly work in this part of the continuum and the next: universal supports available to many students. Instructions can be offered in spoken, written and visual forms. Students may have flexible ways to demonstrate learning, with movement breaks or sensory tools available when useful.
Targeted support responds to a more specific need. For example, a small group might benefit from key vocabulary being introduced before a lesson, or from additional organisation support during independent work.
Individual adjustments respond to the particular student’s access and participation needs. Examples might include a quieter workspace for an assessment, additional processing time, a personalised task breakdown or another way to demonstrate learning. What is appropriate depends on the student and the context, informed by consultation and evidence.
Targeted supports and individual adjustments are planned with the student, family and school team. An early career teacher contributes knowledge and observations and involves the people responsible for those decisions.
Look back at the design check. What is available to everyone? What might need to be targeted? What needs individual adjustment? Academic adjustments sit within this wider picture. A diagnosis alone doesn’t tell us which adjustment will help. Connect the response with your observations, the day map, and student and family knowledge, then review it through use and feedback.

### Slide 20

Reasonable adjustments are changes or supports that help students access and participate in education in a way that works for them. They should be individualised, build on strengths, remove barriers, and be practical and effective. Informed decisions and review help us find out whether they are enabling the student.
The diagram places adjustments within the wider work of environmental design and Universal Design for Learning. It also names the four N C C D levels of adjustment: quality differentiated teaching practice, supplementary, substantial and extensive.
Keep two things distinct. Reasonable adjustments are the changes and supports provided. The N C C D is the process for collecting information about adjustments for students with disability. Evidence of what has been provided and monitored matters; a diagnostic label by itself doesn’t choose an adjustment or tell the whole story.
Within curriculum learning, we want support to build capability, confidence, self-advocacy and independence. The question is whether students can access and participate, with support that empowers them. For formal requirements and decisions, use current official guidance and your school or system processes.
