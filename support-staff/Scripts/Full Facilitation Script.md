### Slide 12

The social model helps us put that curiosity to work. On this slide, we're using an environment-first lens. We look for barriers in the place, the task, the routine and the expectations. Then we ask what can change around the student.

Here's an imaginary classroom example. A class is gathering for a group activity. The usual expectation is that everyone sits close together, in the same space, for the whole activity. One student finds that arrangement difficult.

Before deciding what the student needs to do differently, we can look at what this routine asks of them. How crowded is the space? What choices are there about where to sit? Is sitting in that particular place necessary to take part in the activity?

We might discuss another place to sit, or a different way of joining, with the student and teacher. Those are possibilities to investigate with the student. We haven't established what will help just by coming up with an option.

Changing an environment won't make every difficulty disappear. It gives us somewhere useful to begin, with barriers and supports that we can notice, discuss and review.

### Slide 4

Many of us learnt five senses: touch, vision, taste, hearing and smell. This workshop uses eight. The other three are proprioception, vestibular sense and interoception.

Touch includes information about texture, pressure and temperature. Vision gives information about things such as colour, shape, depth and position. Gustatory and oral experience includes taste, texture, temperature and the sensation of chewing. Hearing includes pitch, loudness, distance and familiar sounds. Smell can also be closely connected with memory.

Proprioception gives us information about where our body is in space, drawing on signals from our muscles and joints. Pressure or movement may be something a student seeks for that input. The vestibular sense relates to balance and movement, including the speed and direction of head movement. Rocking or spinning may serve a regulating purpose for some students.

Interoception relates to signals from inside the body. We'll return to it later in the session.

These senses work together. A single classroom experience can involve several at once. The three less familiar senses can be especially useful to consider when something about a student's experience isn't yet clear to us.

Keep these as possibilities to explore, rather than explanations we can assign by watching alone. Students will differ. There is also an interoception webinar in the Positive Partnerships resources if you want to follow up.

### Slide 9

This slide introduces four sensory response patterns from occupational therapist Winnie Dunn's framework. You may encounter this language in an occupational therapy report or a Sensory Profile School Companion questionnaire. The patterns are useful lenses, rather than fixed types of person.

Registration describes a pattern of noticing less sensory information or missing cues that others notice. Seeking describes actively looking for more input, perhaps through movement, touch or sound.

Sensitivity describes noticing sensory input readily and being affected by it, without necessarily taking action to reduce it. Avoiding describes actively trying to reduce input, perhaps by moving away from a crowd or covering the ears.

The slide also uses under-responsive and over-responsive language, and distinguishes more passive responses from active ways of changing the input. Keep that distinction connected to the student's experience, rather than treating the labels as a judgement.

A student may show different patterns for different senses. Their responses may also change with the context, the load and the day. We are looking at nervous-system responses, not simply a decision to cooperate or avoid a task.

So use the framework to sharpen your questions. What input is present? What has the student communicated? What pattern might be worth exploring? An observation gives us a starting point, not certainty about what is happening inside.

### Slide 3

Our central model is the independence ladder: scaffold, don't substitute. Let's walk through the five rungs.
Doing for means the adult does the task. Helping means the adult does part of it with the student. Prompting means the adult gives a cue and the student does it. Many support staff have been thanked for being the person a student relies on. There is care in that work. Here we are asking how that care can help the student need a little less adult support over time.
The ladder is a planning prompt, not an automatic timetable for removing help. Different tasks, settings and days can need different levels. Needed support can stay, change or return.
