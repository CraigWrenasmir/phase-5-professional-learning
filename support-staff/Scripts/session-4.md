### Slide 3

Our central model is the independence ladder: scaffold, don't substitute. Let's walk through the five rungs.
Doing for means the adult does the task. Helping means the adult does part of it with the student. Prompting means the adult gives a cue and the student does it. Coaching means the adult checks in from a distance. On their own means the student does it without the adult doing or prompting that step.
Take the writing-task example from the guide. At first, an adult gets the book out, finds the page and writes the date. Next, the adult finds the page and the student writes the date. A further step might be pointing to the book and the first step on the board. Coaching could mean checking in from across the room once the student has started. Eventually, the student may use a first-step card without the adult beside them.
In a secondary computer lesson, the same idea could move from opening the file for a student to the student following accessible written steps. A student can be doing something independently while still using a useful tool.
Many support staff have been thanked for being the person a student relies on. There is care in that work. Here we are asking how that care can help the student need a little less adult support over time.
The ladder is a planning prompt, not an automatic timetable for removing help. Different tasks, settings and days can need different levels. Needed support can stay, change or return. Fading is planned with the teacher and student. Think of one support you gave this week. Where did it sit on this ladder? Pause for a brief reflection, or an optional example with colleagues.

### Slide 9

These are small moves you can use alongside the teacher's plan and the student's agreed supports. The first is a two-minute warning before a change. 'Two minutes, then we pack up' may be shown on a timer or card if that makes the information accessible.
The second is fewer words when stress appears. Shorten the sentence, offer one piece of information at a time and wait, rather than filling the space with more instructions.
The third is making the first step visible. That could be a short written step on the desk, or the first question highlighted on a page. The useful part is information the student can return to, instead of needing an adult to repeat it.
The fourth is a short observation note: what you saw, when, what came before and what helped. Pass it to the teacher through the school's agreed process, keeping observation separate from interpretation.
Our fifth move is designed distance, which has its own slide shortly. All five connect to the morning's thinking about transitions, processing and communication. A prompt that is available in the environment can sometimes replace one delivered repeatedly by a person.
As you consider each move, notice whether it is already part of your practice or something to discuss using next week. Keep the focus on what fits your brief and the student's agreed support.

### Slide 10

Predictability means access to information about routine and change. It doesn't mean making every day identical or promising that nothing unexpected will happen.
How much information in the day is spoken, implied or held by adults? When a plan changes, does the student find out only when you tell them? Could they return to the information themselves?
The four questions are: what is happening, what is changing, what comes next, and what can I do if the plan changes? Think of an assembly moved earlier, a relief teacher, or a lesson moved to a different room. How is that change shown? Is the student's support up to date? Can they ask a question or signal that they need more information?
Give information early enough to be useful, in a form the student can access. That can reduce reliance on an adult explaining every change while still leaving help available. Return to your audit across settings: where might information be lost at a move or handover?

### Slide 11

Designed distance asks us to consider where we stand. Sometimes, without anyone choosing it, an adult becomes the person always beside a student. Peers talk to the adult or go around them, and the teacher passes things through the adult. The student's contact with peers and the teacher can gradually shrink.
That pattern can grow from care, and from being asked to stay close. The question is how to make room for the student's own first try and for other relationships, while keeping needed help available.
The guide gives examples: setting up partner reading and then checking in from the next table; helping a pair begin a practical task and then stepping to the side; staying within sight while a peer game gets going. These are possibilities to consider with the teacher, not fixed positions everyone should use.
Some students need close support at particular times, including for safety, distress, personal care or health needs. Follow the student's agreed plan and school policy. Designed distance means planning where support is useful, rather than simply withdrawing it.

### Slide 12

A visual support should clarify something the student needs to find, understand, remember or communicate. Start with that purpose before choosing a tool. Objects, photographs, written words, symbols, checklists, maps and schedules are possible formats. A support isn't automatically accessible because it is visual. Check its meaning with the student, keep it available where it is used and remove unnecessary detail.
The same question matters for social stories: what is this for? The guide contrasts a story asking a student to look at people when they speak with a story helping them understand a fire drill or an excursion. One measures how typical the student looks. The other can support understanding, safety and access, particularly when made with the student in their own interests and words.
Many people have created these supports with care. We're reviewing their purpose and usefulness, rather than asking anyone to rewrite one during this activity. Changes to a student's supports go through the teacher and school processes.
The four review questions are also on page nineteen. Does it help the student, or help the student look typical? Was it made with the student? Does it match what is actually happening? Could the student use it without you?
Choose one support you use and think through those questions. Keep the student's communication system available throughout; no spoken answer does not mean no understanding or preference. Pause briefly if you want to discuss your example.

Now apply a Universal Design for Learning lens. Make important information clear and available. Offer workable ways to participate and respond. Support engagement while retaining meaningful challenge.

### Slide 18

Support is a continuum, rather than a single intervention. Start with an inclusive environment: predictable routines, clear expectations and different ways of participating that are accepted and valued.
Universal supports make access more workable for everyone. Examples include information available in more than one form, movement opportunities and different ways to show learning. Targeted supports respond to a more specific need, such as pre-teaching vocabulary for a small group or providing an organisation checklist.
Individual adjustments respond to a particular student's access and participation needs. A plan might include a quieter workspace, additional processing time, a task breakdown or another way to demonstrate learning. The teacher and school work through the appropriate planning and consultation processes.
Support staff may help deliver support at every layer and notice how it works across the day. Your day map, audit and factual observations can contribute to review. The decision follows the person's access needs and the evidence; diagnosis alone does not tell us which adjustment will help.
Think of the support you give. What is available to everyone? What is targeted? What is individually adjusted? Keep your student's experience at the centre of those questions.

### Slide 19

This slide places reasonable adjustments within layers of inclusive design. It starts with environmental design and Universal Design for Learning, then names quality differentiated teaching practice, supplementary, substantial and extensive adjustments as the four N C C D levels of adjustment.
For this workshop, reasonable adjustments are changes or supports that help students access and participate in education in a way that works for them. They should be individualised, strengths based and practical, removing barriers and enabling participation.
Keep the distinction clear. Adjustments are the support actions. The N C C D is the process for collecting information about adjustments. The teacher and school plan and review those decisions. You help deliver many of the supports day to day and contribute what you notice.
The aim includes capability, confidence, self-advocacy and independence. Follow the consultation, documentation and review processes in your school or system, and use current official guidance for detailed questions.
